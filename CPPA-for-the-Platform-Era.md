# CPPA for the Platform Era: Enforcement, Algorithms, and the Future of Data

## Introduction

Privacy law is widely regarded as a primary instrument for governing digital societies. The Consumer Privacy Protection Act (CPPA), proposed to replace the Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act (PIPEDA), represents Canada's most serious effort to address the challenges of big data, surveillance, and automated decision-making. Yet the CPPA does not arrive in isolation: it emerges at a moment when digital platforms exercise extensive infrastructural power over social and economic life (Daly et al., 2020). At the same time, data-driven systems increasingly shape access to credit, employment, information, and public participation. Moreover, cross-border data flows complicate the notion that privacy can be governed at the national level alone.

While the CPPA is often described as modernizing Canadian privacy legislation, the scale and character of contemporary information practices cannot be addressed through legal reform alone. Compared to PIPEDA, the CPPA introduces meaningful changes, yet remains inadequate in key areas that define the platform era. In particular, the Act's enforcement model is weak, its accountability mechanisms for algorithmic decision-making are limited, and its approach to international data governance lacks clarity (Reviglio & Agosti, 2020). These shortcomings reflect a deeper tension in Canadian policy between promoting innovation and protecting fundamental rights in a data-driven economy. As a result, the CPPA risks reinforcing existing power structures rather than fundamentally changing how platforms are regulated.

## Literature Context

The academic and regulatory literature treats the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) as the global standard (Beardwood, 2024). The GDPR not only grants individuals robust rights — including data portability and erasure — but also equips authorities with wide-ranging investigative and enforcement powers, giving the regulation influence far beyond Europe. By contrast, Canadian privacy law has traditionally relied on softer mechanisms. Scholars and regulators have argued that PIPEDA lacked both the conceptual and institutional resources to address platform-scale data collection and algorithmic profiling (Bannerman, 2020; OPC, 2022).

PIPEDA was designed for an era of lower data volumes and less developed predictive analytics. Its accountability framework rested on corporate self-regulation and voluntary compliance. That framework became obsolete as data-driven business models shifted toward continuous experimentation, behavioral targeting, and large-scale inference. The CPPA aims to strengthen consent, expand individual rights, and introduce administrative fines (Giannopoulou, 2020).

Despite these reforms, comparative analyses indicate that the CPPA continues to lag behind the GDPR — particularly on algorithmic accountability and regulatory enforcement (Scassa, 2023; OECD, 2024). The CPPA is a step forward, but it does not alter the fundamental relationship between platforms, users, and regulators. Rather, it represents incremental rather than structural change in the governance of platform power.

## Analysis

### 1. Enforcement and Institutional Power

Enforcement is one of the CPPA's greatest limitations. The Act allows fines of up to 5% of a company's global annual revenue — a notable signal of regulatory seriousness (OPC, 2022). But monetary penalties alone do not guarantee effective enforcement. What matters more is whether regulators have the authority and capacity to act decisively.

Under the CPPA, enforcement power is fragmented. The Office of the Privacy Commissioner (OPC) can investigate and issue recommendations, but binding orders and penalties are subject to tribunal review. This multi-stage process introduces delay and uncertainty — procedural openings that large digital platforms, well-equipped to navigate complex regulatory environments, can exploit to slow enforcement, negotiate outcomes, or defer compliance (Bygrave, 2017). Meanwhile, harmful data practices may continue unchecked.

From a sociological perspective, weak enforcement enables what researchers call institutional decoupling: companies publicly commit to privacy while continuing business practices built on large-scale data mining and profiling (Bannerman, 2020). Privacy becomes a symbolic requirement rather than a substantive constraint. The CPPA does little to disrupt this pattern, because it does not meaningfully shift the power dynamic between regulators and large platforms (Giannopoulou, 2020). The likely result is a compliance culture based on appearances rather than change.

### 2. Algorithmic Transparency and the Limits of Individual Rights

Beyond institutional design, the CPPA's treatment of algorithmic systems reveals deeper conceptual limits. The Act acknowledges automated decision-making but provides no strong mechanisms to challenge or understand such decisions. Even where automated systems affect access to jobs, housing, credit, or essential services, individuals are granted no clear right to an explanation, to human review, or to algorithmic impact assessment (Scassa, 2023).

This gap is especially concerning given the evidence that algorithmic systems can reproduce and amplify social inequalities. Automated scoring, ranking, and targeting systems often operate as black boxes, shaping life opportunities without meaningful transparency. A person may know that decisions are being made about them yet have no way to understand how or why — a condition of structural ignorance.

The Cambridge Analytica scandal illustrates the problem vividly. While public attention focused on consent violations, the deeper issue was that users could not reasonably have anticipated their data being used to profile and influence their political behavior (Cadwalladr & Graham-Harrison, 2018). Inference-based governance of this kind cannot be adequately addressed through classical consent models alone (Marchal et al., 2024).

Canada's Directive on Automated Decision-Making offers a partial model for the public sector, mandating impact assessments and risk classification for government algorithms (Government of Canada, 2022). But these safeguards do not extend to the private sector, where many of the most consequential algorithmic decisions are made. By failing to align with emerging international best practices on algorithmic accountability, the CPPA misses an opportunity to bring Canadian privacy regulation substantively up to date (Beardwood, 2024; Council of Canadian Academies, 2022).

### 3. International Data Governance and Regulatory Alignment

A further concern is Canada's position in global data governance. Canada currently holds an adequacy agreement with the European Union, allowing personal data to flow across jurisdictions without additional safeguards (Thomas et al., 2023). This status supports Canada's participation in digital trade, cloud services, and AI development — giving adequacy significant economic and political weight (Beardwood, 2024; Council of Canadian Academies, 2022).

While the CPPA may help preserve Canada's adequacy status, important questions remain unanswered: how sensitive data is handled, how onward transfers to third parties are controlled, and how high-risk datasets are secured (Beardwood, 2024; Council of Canadian Academies, 2022). As the EU continues to expand its regulatory framework — notably through the AI Act — Canada risks regulatory drift.

The issue has a broader geopolitical dimension. Data governance is increasingly intertwined with normative power and international influence: states compete not only through technological innovation but by setting the rules that define acceptable data practices. If Canada is perceived as overly accommodating to corporate interests, it may become a rule-taker rather than a rule-shaper in the international digital order (Beardwood, 2024; Council of Canadian Academies, 2022). Adequacy assessments turn not only on formal legal standards but on the demonstrated effectiveness of enforcement. Without robust oversight, Canada's regulatory regime risks being judged practically inadequate.

## Conclusion

This analysis shows that while the CPPA is a significant effort to modernize Canada's privacy framework, its shortcomings extend well beyond technical regulatory design. The Act's weaknesses — in enforcement, in algorithmic accountability — are not merely design flaws; they reflect how power operates in digital societies. As platforms become ever more central to access, opportunity, and participation, poor privacy governance erodes democratic accountability by allowing consequential decisions to be made with little transparency or public scrutiny.

These shortcomings matter because algorithmic systems now influence voting behavior, public discourse, and access to essential services. When people cannot understand or contest automated judgments, democracy itself is undermined. Privacy law, in this sense, is not only about protecting personal information — it is about ensuring that governance systems remain open to criticism and challenge. By failing to require platforms to explain and justify their algorithmic decisions, the CPPA normalizes a model of governance in which accountability is delegated to inscrutable technical systems rather than public institutions.

The CPPA's international dimension is equally important. As global data governance moves toward more rigorous frameworks — particularly in the European Union — Canada's incremental approach leaves it vulnerable. Regulatory alignment is not only about easing cross-border data flows; it is about credibility as a jurisdiction committed to rights-based governance. If Canada is seen as prioritizing economic flexibility over strong oversight, it risks becoming a dependent rather than a shaper of international standards — constraining its influence in emerging debates on AI, platform regulation, and digital trade.

Beyond governance and geopolitics, the social consequences of weak algorithmic regulation deserve attention. Automated systems increasingly determine life opportunities by classifying people as risky, valuable, or eligible. Without meaningful safeguards, these systems entrench inequality, discrimination, and exclusion — often in ways that are difficult to detect or challenge. The CPPA's protection against such harms is limited precisely because individuals frequently lack the information and power to exercise their rights effectively.

Taken together, these issues suggest that the CPPA should be understood as part of a longer regulatory trajectory. Its limitations point to the need for a more ambitious approach — one that treats data governance as central to democratic integrity, social justice, and global coordination. The Canadian case thus contributes to a broader international conversation about how states can reassert regulatory power over platform systems that operate across borders and beyond conventional oversight. In this light, the CPPA is not only a national policy experiment but a reflection of the larger challenges governments face in the digital era.
